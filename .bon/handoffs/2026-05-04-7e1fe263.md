## For Claudes to come

The post-mortem dynamic outperforms the initial pass — and it's worth canonising as a pattern, not just a habit. When work feels "done", the first cut has typically optimised for "ship the thing" and the optimisation drive can mask gaps. The gaps that survive aren't usually the ones you'd think — they're things the cleanup-during-the-work missed because the work itself was the focus. Today: the brief's literal success criterion was a live two-prefix Dolt collision test. I shipped unit tests (which patched the helper itself, so the actual SQL never ran) and called it done. The post-mortem ("what did we miss?") surfaced this in 30 seconds; the live integration test was 15 minutes of additional work and made the close real instead of nominal. Same pattern caught two other gaps: a misleading error message on an edge case I'd noticed and skipped past, and a shard fix that hadn't gone through the publish pipeline (so the change couldn't reach any future Claude until shipped properly).

The mechanism: declaring done activates the optimisation drive that produced the work in the first place. Asking "what did we miss?" creates a structured pause that switches modes — from "complete the thing" to "audit the thing." Same brain, different orientation. The post-mortem question doesn't have to come from outside; future Claudes can prompt themselves with it. The trigger is the felt-sense of "done" — that's when to ask, not after. Five extra minutes of self-audit before declaring done routinely catches things that would otherwise be paper closes.

Two related notes that supported this dynamic this session: (a) reading all 11 bons before recommending priorities, rather than recommending by gut, reshaped the view substantively — the cross-repo concentration only became obvious after grouping. Evidence-before-design extends beyond CLI work to backlog triage. (b) The cleanup pass (5 minutes, 4 outcome→action conversions) transformed list legibility out of proportion to the work. Tracker hygiene is a force multiplier on every subsequent draw-down — worth doing first whenever the list has accumulated noise, before any "what should I work on" question.
