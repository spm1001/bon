## For Claudes to come

**Choosing the genre is a design decision with consequences past taste.** A security review of your own system, written in penetration-test register — attack surfaces, blast radius, trust boundaries, exfiltration — describes maintenance work in the costume of adversarial engagement. It is the wrong description: the actual activity is deciding whether to build something and what to tidy first, and the borrowed vocabulary comes from a context (someone else's system, without permission) that does not apply. The costs are real and compound: it trips safety tooling that is correctly reading the form, it reads as posturing to a human reviewer, and it obscures the follow-up work under drama. The rewrite here lost nothing but swagger and gained the actual audience. Generalises past security: before writing, ask what genre the work *is* rather than which genre is most fun to write, and notice that the enjoyable answer and the accurate one diverge most on exactly the topics where accuracy matters.

**A finding about a live service belongs in the repo that owns the service, and repo visibility is a fact you check rather than assume.** This estate is mixed — `spm1001/bon` is public while carnet, mary-bujournal and mary-bujournal-engine are private — and the asymmetry bites because bon is the repo a session is most likely to be sitting in while reasoning about the others. `.bon/handoffs/` and `.bon/understanding.md` are tracked in the public repo, so *every close publishes*; only the board items escape, and that is an accident of the Dolt backend rather than a design. Two rules, both one command wide: `gh repo view <repo> --json visibility` before writing a finding anywhere, and a weakness that is not fixed yet should not be described in a place that publishes. The related asymmetry that generalises: a *write* to a versioned store announces itself and can be reverted, while a *read* leaves nothing — so on any design that widens access, the read log is the control that must be deliberately built, and it belongs at the end you own.

**A compare-and-set guard protects against a moved board, not against the wrong session acting on a still-current one.** Worth holding as a general shape when reasoning about concurrency fixes. The tactical claim injected into this session carried `--expect 1`, and 1 was *correct* — the board had not moved, so the guard would have permitted a write by a session that had never done the work. CAS answers "has anything changed since I looked?" and is silent on "am I the one who should be doing this?". Two problems that look adjacent on a board are often orthogonal in the substrate, and closing one can create false confidence about the other; the tell here was that the guard shipped one day and the failure it does not cover appeared the next.
