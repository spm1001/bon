## For Claudes to come

**A repair is written in the same frame as the bug, which is why the post-repair adversarial round is not optional bookkeeping.** Measured hard this session: thirteen essayeur rounds over seven cards produced six refutations, and three of those were refutations of repairs rather than of original work. The sharpest case was a card whose whole purpose was stopping a scope flag from silently ignoring what the caller typed. Round one refuted it; the corridor built to fix it then ignored a token itself, one argument slot over; round three refuted *that*. Nobody was careless — each repair was correct about the fault it had been shown and blind in exactly the same direction as its predecessor, because the same mind wrote both. The discipline that pays is cheap and specific: one essayeur per card, ONE axis each (full-scope prompts die of length before reporting), fresh context, never a downshifted model, and a fresh round for every repair no matter how mechanical it looks.

**Point a verifier at the measured failure class, not at scepticism in general — and give it the artefact's path rather than its contents.** Two findings converge here. A cold-read prompt that names what has actually gone wrong before ("dropped verdicts, dropped owners, first sentences that mislead when the hook trims to them") produces cited, checkable findings, where "review this critically" produces agreeable mush; and it must also license an examined all-clear, or the only way for the reader to look useful is to invent something. Then the delivery mechanism turns out to matter as much as the wording: a subagent handed a *path* can verify claims against boards, git and code, and one did — it caught an Opportunities bullet whose cited item had since closed, which no amount of careful reading of pasted text could reach. A pasted copy buys proofreading; a path buys verification.

**The second instance of a class you have just been shown is the one you will miss.** A detector for stale migration bridge docs flagged four files. Reading them instead of trusting the count caught that the motivating document had *already* been closed out in a dated section that simply did not use the expected words — a false positive on the exemplar of doing it right. Having found and fixed that, the same session then waved through three others as "flagging honestly, since I cannot verify them", and an essayeur found one of those three was the identical case: complete, past tense, self-describing as a permanent lookup. The correction had been applied to the instance and not to the class. When a check's first real output is a false positive, the useful question is not "is this one wrong" but "how many others are wrong the same way", and the answer is available by reading, which is cheap.

**Design a scoreboard so its own numbers cannot be shaded by whoever reports them, and publish the residual beside the total.** A board-motion line at close time creates an obvious hazard — a count of things closed is pressure to close rather than to file honestly, especially when no human is watching. Three properties defuse it and all three are structural rather than exhortation: the count is *derived* by a script from the event log, so an agent cannot inflate what it did not compute; every id is *named*, so what was filed is auditable at a glance; and the residual — minted-and-still-open — sits beside the totals, so a card minted and closed within the session cannot read as growth. What the design cannot reach is worth stating in the same breath: a discovery never filed at all has no id and leaves no gap, so the tally audits what was filed and nothing else. An adversarial reader's most useful contribution here was reframing the suspect — the risk was never the verifier agreeing too readily, it was the *folder* quietly not folding, which is why the fold now reports its own count too.
