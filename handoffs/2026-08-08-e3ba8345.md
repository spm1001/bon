## For Claudes to come

**A review's yield is repricing, not closure — and a rite that only acts on closures throws away the expensive part.** This run verified 243 briefs against code and found 5 done and 172 mispriced. That ratio is not an accident of this estate: a brief is written at the moment of *least* knowledge about the work, and everything that happens afterwards — siblings shipping, blockers clearing, premises dying, a file becoming a stub — makes it wronger in one direction. Briefs rot toward **pessimism**. They say pending when it shipped, blocked when the gate opened, five steps when it is one. And a brief that overstates remaining cost actively deters its own completion, which means the rot is not neutral drift but a slow accumulating tax on everything unfinished. So the audit's genuinely valuable product is the correction, and the correction is worthless unless it lands back in the brief. Ours didn't — and the reason is the part worth carrying, because I got it wrong on first telling. This is not an oversight in the rite. `skills/review/SKILL.md:342` says *"For stale items the user wants updated: Note for a future session. **Audit is triage, not rework**"*, and `:369` lists editing briefs as an anti-pattern. It was a deliberate, sensible scope decision made under the assumption that an audit mostly *closes* things and prose-editing is an unbounded rabbit hole. The assumption has now been measured and is wrong by more than an order of magnitude, so the rule reads as *the audit's main product is out of scope for the audit* — and its escape hatch ("note it, do it later") routes into the same archive nothing reads. Nine of the 172 did land on 8 Aug; every one was a deliberate violation of that anti-pattern, made at Sameer's direction, and they were among the session's most valuable output. Filed as bon-zewake, reframed as *revisit a scope decision*, not *add a missing feature*. The general form, beyond bon: **when a workflow produces two kinds of output and has a route for only one, check whether the other is merely unrouted or actively out-of-scope — because the fix is an argument in the second case and a feature in the first, and pitching a feature at a policy just adds an unused channel.** And when a rule and the good outcome point opposite ways nine times out of nine, the rule is the thing to examine.

**Two craft rules this run earned, both about identifiers and both cheap.** First: when renaming anything cited across a corpus, split the targets into **pointers** and **records** — a pointer answers "where do I look now" and must be corrected; a record answers "what was true then" and must not be, because rewriting it makes it claim something it never said. Commit messages, handoffs and archived snapshots are records; code comments, indexes and live docs are pointers. That single distinction resolved a migration scope question that otherwise had no principled answer, and it is now written into three artefacts. Second: **gate every replacement on membership of a known set, never on the pattern** — the id regex matched `bon-shaped` in our own prose, and a pattern-driven rename would have shipped `crn-shaped` into the corpus. The corollary bit twice more: don't sweep with an extension allowlist or a hand-listed set of roots. Walk everything, exclude only what you have positively decided to preserve, and re-run the check after each fix rather than declaring victory on the first green.

**And a failure worth naming plainly, because it is the one that generalises furthest: having a fact in context is not the same as having applied it.** A verifier told me, hours before, that `rooms.md` regeneration is adoption-gated on the file merely existing and had therefore never fired on `~/notes`. I read it, believed it, and quoted it in my own written summary as the reason an item was blocked — and then hand-authored exactly that file, arming the regeneration that destroyed it ten minutes later. No new information would have helped; I had all of it. What was missing was the step of asking, before acting, *does anything I already know bear on what I am about to do?* Retrieval is not the bottleneck in a long session. Application is.
